Candidate and job matching for recruiters

Tracefit architecture

The same person and the same job are described differently, at different times, by sources of different authority. Tracefit keeps every fact with its source, date and exact words, then asks two questions about each person: can they do this job, and how good a fit are they. Every point of every score traces back to a quote.

How a record becomes a recommendation

Six steps, always in this order. Each hands a plain result to the next, so any one can change without touching the others.

Two questions for every person

First, can they do this job? Required rules answer pass, fail or unknown, and no score can buy past them: a missing license or a declined shift is not something a better résumé makes up for. Then, how good a fit are they? Only people who pass are ranked, by a score built from six named dimensions.

Setting 25Dated experience in the job's care setting, or at an employer the client names. Undated: 70.
Skills 20The tools the job asks for. A candidate's own "I have not used it" wins; preferred skills count half.
Depth 15Documented years against twice the minimum. Claimed years count at most half.
Evidence 15Minus 30 for a claim the records do not support or a newer message that could not be read; minus 15 for a stale answer.
Readiness 15Start date, schedule and shifts met, and how recently the candidate replied.
Pay 10At or under the approved rate scores 100; 10 points off for each 1% above.

On the sample, the ten people who pass every gate for the home-health job score 81 to 93, each gap traceable to one dimension. Under the earlier count-the-rules score, seven of them tied at 100.

What is kept, and what is computed

This split is what makes the rest of the design safe. Only two things are the record. Everything a recruiter sees is computed from them and can be thrown away.

Six rules that keep it correct across machines

  1. One person, one worker. Identity is settled before routing, on an index of emails and explicit references, so two records of one person always reach the same place.
  2. Unknown is not "no". Only a stated, different profession sets a person aside. People with no stated profession stay in every worker's pool.
  3. Order per person and per job. A late arrival is still placed correctly, because every fact carries its own date.
  4. An event applied twice changes nothing. Events carry an id; a row is written by its key.
  5. A row, its change message and its outbox entry are written together, so a recruiter is never told about a move that was not saved.
  6. Every row records the settings version that produced it. A rebuild writes the new version beside the old one and switches when complete.

Rules 3 and 4 hold in the code today. Rules 1, 2, 5 and 6 describe how the unbuilt parts must behave; rule 2 is already how the profession index works inside one process.

A new job is the expensive event, at about 0.15 ms per person in its profession. It happens once per job and off the page's path. Split between worker processes on one machine it shortens, with an identical list: at 100,000 people on a 16-core cloud machine, 7.1 s on one worker and 1.17 s on sixteen. That is measured; across several machines it is not.

Safety nets

  • Unread rather than misread. A sentence with a negation, doubt or question the reader cannot place never yields a yes. A sweep of 124 reworded sentences first found 57 unsafe readings and 10 of 11 contact requests missed; now there are none. A newer message that cannot be read turns an older yes into a question.
  • One person, one id. Every person is keyed by source and record id. Records join only on an explicit reference or the same email, never a name or a shared info@ mailbox. Two people with one name stay two, and the page shows which record each is.
  • Bad data never stops matching. A missing file, broken record, unreadable or impossible date, unknown event or duplicate is worked around and listed for a person to fix.
  • The model reader cannot raise anyone. Against a stand-in that says yes to everything, no false yes gets through, no contact request is lifted, and nobody's tier rises. Built, switched off, never run against a real model.
  • The checker (python3 -m shortlist audit) re-derives each tier and score and checks every quote, permission and the list order. Clean on all 176 sample results, both client profiles, every scenario and with the lying model on; a test plants twelve kinds of mistake and each is caught.
  • The worked example cannot drift. It is generated by the tool, and a test fails if the document stops matching.

Order to build the rest

Each step is useful on its own. More workers come last because the measurements say one process already carries 100,000 people.

  1. Store evidence and claims in a database. A restart stops re-reading every document.
  2. Store rows in a table and read pages from it. Memory no longer grows with open jobs.
  3. Add the queue and one real connector. Replay a real day of events and record the time from event to shortlist.
  4. Add sign-in, the change log and settings versions. The ownership rule can then use the signed-in recruiter.
  5. Add the nightly date event.
  6. Split workers by profession, only if step 3 shows a new job takes too long.

Tied to the sample, and first to revisit with real data: the text reader's 55 rules, the six meanings it recognises in job notes, the vocabulary, and the dimension weights, which are reasoned defaults until they can be calibrated on submissions and placements. The synthetic people measure speed and memory only; they say nothing about match quality.
